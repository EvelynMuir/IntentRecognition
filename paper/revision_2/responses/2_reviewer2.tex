We thank the reviewer for acknowledging the improvements in methodological detail, evaluation, and presentation, and for stating the remaining concerns so precisely. All four are addressed with changes in the manuscript, summarized below.}

\cresp{Nevertheless, there are still some serious scientific concerns. There is still a lack of theoretical justification of functionally separate two types of ambiguity.}
{\gist{Sec.~\ref{sec:decoupling_principle} has been rebuilt around an exact identity plus one derivation per module, and Sec.~\ref{sec:quadrants} adds an experiment that tests what they predict.}

We agree, and have rebuilt the analysis on an exact footing. Sec.~\ref{sec:decoupling_principle} now derives the exact identity $H(Y_c\mid Z)=H(Y_c\mid X)+I(Y_c;X\mid Z)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:entropy_decomp}), which separates irreducible annotator disagreement from label-relevant information the representation discards. Each module is then characterized on its own terms. SLR-C is stated through the conditional mutual information it supplies (Eq.~\eqref{eq:cmi_prior}) and the margin it shifts inside the screened candidate set (Eq.~\eqref{eq:margin_identity}). UTD is stated as learning using privileged information, whose mean-squared-error-optimal weight decreases in annotator agreement and so motivates the gate $1-\omega$ (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:opt_gate}--\eqref{eq:gate_grad}).

It establishes that the two ambiguities are functionals of different objects, the annotation conditional and the representation map, and therefore admit corrective mechanisms of different type. Whether that pays off is empirical, so we added an experiment to test it. The new $2\times2$ stratification below confirms the prediction on the semantic axis, where SLR-C gains only in the high-semantic-ambiguity cells, and shows UTD inert wherever annotator agreement is high, with its only significant gain where both ambiguities coincide. The full model does not dominate the doubly-ambiguous cell, so we claim the predicted localization of the two effects rather than a gain from composing them.

\cresp{Moreover, the evaluation is limited to the single benchmark dataset that makes it impossible to assess generalization ability of the approach.}
{\gist{We agree, and the manuscript now carries a cross-dataset study on two label-distribution benchmarks.}

Sec.~\ref{sec:flickrldl} evaluates FDIL on Flickr-LDL ($11{,}150$ images) and Emotion6 ($1{,}980$ images), each on its untouched official test split over five seeds with paired $t$-tests. These vary a different axis than a further multi-label dataset would. Label distribution learning replaces independent per-class decisions with a point on the probability simplex, so the threshold search disappears and only the two functional roles remain.

\rh{Results.} On Flickr-LDL, FDIL significantly improves rank correlation and the divisiveness error but significantly degrades Clark distance. On Emotion6 the only reliable change is Clark distance, the very metric it loses on Flickr-LDL. The margins are small and Emotion6's $594$-image test split has little power at this effect size, so we do not present either dataset as a new state of the art for label distribution learning. The recent LDL-DVS objective~\cite{lu2026divisiveness}, run at its default weight on identical features, splits, and seeds, stays essentially identical to the matched baseline under these strong frozen CLIP features, which also limits what that comparison can establish.

\rh{What we do take from the study.} The division of labour replicates on both datasets. SLR-C moves the pointwise-fit metrics (KLD, cosine, $\mu$) and only those, while gated UTD moves rank correlation and leaves KLD no better. That separation holds where the two modules cannot share a fitted threshold, which is the most plausible way it could otherwise have been an artefact. Table~\ref{tab:ldl_letter} below gives the seed dispersion and per-metric $p$-values that the manuscript table omits for space.
